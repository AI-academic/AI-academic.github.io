Toronto Metropolitan University's Leadership Task Force on Generative AI, launched in October 2025, published its report on October 1. Its recommendations are organised around five areas: policy, guidelines and governance; technological infrastructure; training and professional development; learning, teaching and student success; and scholarly, research and creative activity. The central structural choice is to adapt and strengthen TMU's existing policies to account for AI rather than create a standalone AI policy, under three guiding principles: equitable access and inclusion, ethical and responsible use, and compliance with data privacy and security obligations. The university says it will keep assessing and critiquing AI's effects on society as it applies the technology, prioritising uses that amplify human intellect and agency over transactional efficiency.

This is the most directly useful item of the week for McMaster's AI Strategy work, and the amend-existing-policy choice is the part worth examining closely. It is the opposite bet from a standalone framework: lower visibility, but it embeds obligations in instruments that already have owners, compliance routes and review cycles, which is where standalone AI policies tend to fail. TMU is also a close structural comparator — comprehensive, Ontario, similar governance machinery — making its five-pillar split a reasonable benchmark for whether McMaster's own pillars are drawn in the right places.
